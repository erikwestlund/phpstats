The random-intercept walkthrough introduces clustering. Here we allow each subject both a different starting reaction time and a different response to days of sleep deprivation. We use the published sleepstudy data, not synthetic row numbers. The optimizer audit verifies this model against R lme4 and statsmodels under ML and REML.

Observations and matrices

\[ y_i=o_i+x_i^\top\beta+z_i^\top b_{g(i)}+\epsilon_i, \qquad b_g\sim N(0,\Sigma),\quad \epsilon_i\sim N(0,\sigma^2/w_i). \]

Here o is a known offset, w is a positive prior weight, x contains fixed predictors and z contains random predictors. For sleepstudy, o=0, w=1 and both local designs have [1, Days] columns. The ones construct an intercept; Days is observed data. Beta contains the population intercept and slope. Each group’s b contains deviations from those population coefficients, not a second set of observed predictors.

Covariance through a Cholesky factor

Instead of optimizing variance/correlation entries directly, optimize a lower triangular relative factor:

\[ L=\begin{bmatrix}a&0\\c&d\end{bmatrix},\qquad a,d\geq0, \qquad\Sigma=\sigma^2LL^\top =\sigma^2\begin{bmatrix}a^2&ac\\ac&c^2+d^2\end{bmatrix}. \]

This guarantees positive semidefinite covariance, including valid zero-variance or perfect-correlation boundaries. A negative c can represent negative intercept/slope covariance. lme4’s lower-triangle parameter ordering is column-major: [a, c, d]. Several terms concatenate their parameters in supplied term order.

from scipy.linalg import choleskyreference=c['references']['bobyqa']['result']Sigma=np.array(reference['terms'][0]['covariance'])sigma2=reference['residual_variance'];L=cholesky(Sigma/sigma2,lower=True)matrix_tex(L,'L');matrix_tex(sigma2*L@L.T,r'\widehat\Sigma')print('Intercept SD (ms):',np.sqrt(Sigma[0,0]))print('Slope SD (ms/day):',np.sqrt(Sigma[1,1]))print('Intercept/slope correlation:',Sigma[0,1]/np.sqrt(Sigma[0,0]*Sigma[1,1]))

Penalized least squares profiles out beta and random effects

Repeat L once per group to construct block-diagonal Lambda. Write b=Lambda u, where u has spherical covariance sigma²I. Given theta, minimize

\[ q(\theta)=\min_{u,\beta} \left\|W^{1/2}(y-o-X\beta-Z\Lambda u)\right\|^2+\|u\|^2. \]

The first term fits observed reaction times; the second penalizes implausibly large standardized random effects. This is a consequence of their Gaussian likelihood, not an arbitrary regularization parameter chosen by the user. It is the least-squares problem

\[ \begin{bmatrix}W^{1/2}Z\Lambda&W^{1/2}X\\I&0\end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}u\\\beta\end{bmatrix} \approx\begin{bmatrix}W^{1/2}(y-o)\\0\end{bmatrix}. \]

For a selected theta, linear algebra finds u and beta. The nonlinear optimizer searches theta only. We solve with QR; coefficients do not come from explicitly inverting a normal-equation matrix.

ML and REML are different objectives

With C=W⁻¹+Z Lambda Lambdaᵀ Zᵀ, the profiled Gaussian ML scale is

\[\hat\sigma^2_{ML}=q/n.\]

The negative profiled marginal log-likelihood is

\[ -\ell_{ML}(\theta)=\tfrac12\left[ n\{\log(2\pi)+1+\log(q/n)\}+\log|C| \right]. \]

REML adjusts for estimating the p fixed coefficients. It uses

\[ \hat\sigma^2_{REML}=q/(n-p), \]

and the restricted criterion

\[ -\ell_{REML}(\theta)=\tfrac12\left[ (n-p)\{\log(2\pi)+1+\log(q/(n-p))\} +\log|C|+\log|X^\top C^{-1}X| \right]. \]

REML changes the optimization objective, not merely the final denominator. Its extra determinant accounts for fixed-effect estimation. Both determinants come from triangular QR factors in our implementation. Scaling fixed columns requires restoring their determinant contribution, even though scaling does not change the fitted mean. REML likelihood values should not compare different fixed-effect designs.

Fixed-effect uncertainty

At the fitted variance components, the plug-in covariance is

\[\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(\hat\beta)=\hat\sigma^2(X^\top C^{-1}X)^{-1}.\]

The fixed triangular QR block supplies this through triangular solves. Standard errors are square roots of its diagonal. Our mixed-model z tests and 95% intervals are normal Wald approximations; this does not supply finite-group Student t degrees of freedom or variance-component confidence intervals.

R_inv=solve_triangular(qr_fixed,np.eye(x.shape[1]))cov_beta=reml_variance*R_inv@R_inv.Tmatrix_tex(cov_beta,r'\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(\hat\beta)')print('Standard errors:',np.sqrt(cov_beta.diagonal()))assert np.allclose(cov_beta,reference['covariance'],rtol=1e-8,atol=1e-8)

Four grouping levels: which observations share variation?

The same construction handles multiple nested terms. For repeated assessments within students, classes, schools and districts,

\[ y_i=x_i^\top\beta+d_{D(i)}+s_{S(i)}+c_{C(i)}+t_{T(i)}+\epsilon_i. \]

Each term has its own Gaussian variance and is independent of the other terms. Conditional on the fixed design, two different observations share covariance only through common groups:

\[ \operatorname{Cov}(y_i,y_j\mid X) =\tau_D^2\mathbf{1}_{D(i)=D(j)} +\tau_S^2\mathbf{1}_{S(i)=S(j)} +\tau_C^2\mathbf{1}_{C(i)=C(j)} +\tau_T^2\mathbf{1}_{T(i)=T(j)},\quad i\ne j. \]

On the diagonal, add the observation variance sigma² (or sigma²/w_i with weights). The grouping IDs must be globally unique within each term: a local school number needs its district in the key, and a local student number needs all its parents. Three grouping terms plus observations are often called a four-level model; here we name the number of grouping terms explicitly.
