1. Meaningful simulated observations

We observe simulated clinic days, with daily visitors, a staffing measure and the number of calls received. These are synthetic counts, not patient records. R seed 20261014 generates 120 days. Visitors are rounded uniform draws between 80 and 300; staffing is visitors/50 plus normal noise with standard deviation 0.8, clipped to 2–10 and rounded to two decimals. Counts are independent draws from

\[y_i\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(\mu_i),\qquad \log\mu_i=0.1+0.005\,\mathrm{visitors}_i-0.12\,\mathrm{staff}_i.\]

Inputs and outcomes are data. The supplied ones column estimates the intercept. Known generating parameters are distinct from fitted estimates, which vary by sample. The outcome is a nonnegative integer; the fitted mean \(\mu_i\) is a positive real number, not a rounded count or a probability. Under the model, \(E[y_i\mid x_i]=\operatorname{Var}(y_i\mid x_i)=\mu_i\).

The log link is \(\eta_i=x_i^\top\beta=\log\mu_i\), so \(\mu_i=e^{\eta_i}\). Holding other predictors fixed, a one-unit increase multiplies the mean by \(e^{\beta_j}\). A negative staffing coefficient here describes a conditional association, not a causal staffing effect. This first API has no exposure/offset argument: a constant exposure is implicit, and these are daily counts, not rates.

2. Likelihood and derivatives

The probability of one count is

\[P(y_i\mid\mu_i)=\frac{e^{-\mu_i}\mu_i^{y_i}}{y_i!}.\]

Taking logs and substituting \(\mu_i=e^{\eta_i}\) gives

\[\ell(\beta)=\sum_i[y_i\eta_i-e^{\eta_i}-\log\Gamma(y_i+1)].\]

For integer counts \(\Gamma(y_i+1)=y_i!\). The factorial term does not change the coefficient optimum, but must remain in reported likelihood and AIC. Rust uses statrs::function::gamma::ln_gamma instead of forming a huge factorial.

Since \(d e^{\eta_i}/d\eta_i=e^{\eta_i}=\mu_i\), the first derivative is \(y_i-\mu_i\). Applying \(d\eta_i/d\beta_j=x_{ij}\) gives

\[g=X^\top(y-\mu),\qquad H=-X^\top W X,\qquad W=\operatorname{diag}(\mu_i).\]

Observed and expected information coincide here: \(I=-H=X^\top W X\). There is no OLS estimate of error variance; Poisson dispersion is fixed at one. Actual count data with overdispersion can have understated conventional standard errors. Robust covariance and negative-binomial models are outside this implementation.

3. Scoring, weighted matrices and SVD

Fisher scoring solves \(I\Delta\beta=g\) and updates coefficients. For Poisson this is also the Newton step, since information equals negative Hessian. To avoid forming and inverting the cross-product for fitting, define column scales \(D\), \(A=XD^{-1}\) and \(\theta=D\beta\). Solve

\[B\Delta\theta\approx q,\quad B=W^{1/2}A,\quad q=W^{-1/2}s, \qquad B=USV^\top,\quad \Delta\theta=VS^{-1}U^\top q.\]

The weighted least-squares normal equations are \(A^\top W A\Delta\theta=A^\top s\). This is the same update in scaled coordinates, with \(\Delta\beta=D^{-1}\Delta\theta\). We display cross-products to understand them; the production solve uses weighted SVD. See the OLS SVD explanation.

Weights and scores change each iteration: this is IRLS. The full increment can overshoot, so a decreasing-likelihood step is halved, at most 30 times. Both a small full coefficient increment and a small likelihood change are required. A tiny damped step alone does not establish convergence; the limit is 100 updates.

4. Covariance and Wald inference

Evaluate information at the final coefficients, then compute

\[\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(\hat\beta)=I(\hat\beta)^{-1},\quad \operatorname{SE}_j=\sqrt{\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(\hat\beta)_{jj}},\quad z_j=\hat\beta_j/\operatorname{SE}_j.\]

The SVD supplies the covariance as \(D^{-1}VS^{-2}V^\top D^{-1}\). Two-sided p-values use \(2P(Z\ge |z_j|)\) and 95% Wald limits use \(\hat\beta_j\pm1.959964\operatorname{SE}_j\). These are asymptotic normal tests, not OLS Student t tests. We do not multiply covariance by RSS/(n-p). R confint.default() supplies equivalent Wald intervals; ordinary GLM confint() uses profile likelihood instead.

5. Likelihood, deviance and AIC

The saturated model sets \(\mu_i=y_i\). Its log-likelihood is generally not zero:

\[\ell_{\mathrm{sat}}=\sum_i[y_i\log y_i-y_i-\log\Gamma(y_i+1)],\qquad D=2(\ell_{\mathrm{sat}}-\ell(\hat\beta)) =2\sum_i[y_i\log(y_i/\hat\mu_i)-(y_i-\hat\mu_i)].\]

For zero counts use \(0\log0=0\), so the deviance contribution is \(2\hat\mu_i\). For \(y_i>0\), Rust writes \(d_i=\eta_i-\log y_i\) and evaluates the contribution as \(2y_i[\operatorname{expm1}(d_i)-d_i]\) to preserve precision near a fitted count. Unlike binary regression, Poisson deviance is not -2 × log-likelihood. An explicit intercept gives null mean \(\bar y\); without it the null mean is one.

AIC is \(2p-2\ell(\hat\beta)\); residual degrees of freedom are \(n-p\), without an estimated dispersion parameter. Predictions are mean counts, and residuals are observed minus fitted. These vectors follow retained input row order.

6. Assumptions, failure modes and experiments

Counts must be finite nonnegative integers and at least one must be positive. An all-zero sample is rejected, including no-intercept designs, as a conservative first-version restriction. Certain predictor patterns have a boundary MLE with coefficients diverging; the fitter rejects nonconvergence or singular information rather than returning a partial fit. It has no exhaustive boundary-existence test. Independent outcomes, equal conditional mean/variance and linear log mean are model assumptions. There are no offsets, exposures, sample weights, grouped trials, robust errors, zero-inflation or negative-binomial alternatives. Try doubling every count while retaining the design: which coefficient should shift, and why?

For both methods the retained design must have n > p and full numerical rank. Missing null/NAN rows are dropped with a warning unless missing: ‘raise’. No partial unconverged result is returned. See the contract and the executed three-way comparison.

Rust connections

fit_poisson owns its input vectors and converts them to nalgebra matrices. Borrowed matrix operands remain available for later iterations; iterators construct weights and score residuals. A dedicated loop performs this estimator’s updates. Result and ? propagate validation/numerical failures. The returned struct holds numeric fields; the PHP binding converts those fields into an associative array and adds warning/sample metadata. No generic optimizer or family hierarchy is added.
